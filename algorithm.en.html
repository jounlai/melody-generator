Automatic Generation of Healing Piano Melody
from a Fragment Catalogue and Phrase Structure

A formal description of melody-generator

Created by Jounlai Cho (cho@heuron.com) / Heuron Inc.

This paper describes the design of a system that composes healing piano music in real time, without end. Its central claim is a single one: the beauty of a melody does not arise from deciding one note after another. It becomes controllable only once the problem is split into two layers — a finite set of fragments already guaranteed to be beautiful, and a temporal structure that places them. The first layer is built by drawing 300,000 candidates and selecting 999 of them by stratified sampling against statistics induced from 125 public-domain melodies. The second is formulated as constraint satisfaction over phrase structure, an intensity curve, and voice placement. Generation is deterministic: a six-character seed fixes the piece.

1Problem and approach

The goal is solo piano music that is generated without interruption and that still has an emotional arc within each piece. This imposes two requirements that pull against each other. One is real time: the next piece must be assembled before the current one finishes. The other is global structure: without a design that spans tens of seconds — statement, development, peak, return — no amount of local beauty in the intervals will make the result sound like a piece of music.

Note-by-note generation (a Markov chain, or an autoregressive model) satisfies the first and struggles with the second. Local transition probabilities can decide "the next note", but they do not express "what must be stated now so that the highest note lands sixteen bars from here". This system splits the problem instead.

  1. The fragment layer. Two-bar (eight-beat) melodic fragments are generated in advance, scored for beauty, and catalogued. Every property a fragment must have — interval distribution, filling in after a leap, rhythmic variety — is checked here, once.
  2. The structure layer. At run time, fragments are only chosen and placed. The choice is governed by phrase structure, the intensity curve, and smoothness of connection; individual notes are never touched.

This split reduces the run-time computation to "draw one element from the set of fragments that satisfy the constraints". With 999 fragments and chord compatibility precomputed, assembling a 32-bar piece takes a measured 6.5 milliseconds; converting it to performance events costs about the same again.

2Notation

2.1Pitch space

Melodies are written in running scale degrees, not absolute pitch. Degree d ∈ ℤ takes the tonic as 1, so 8 is an octave above and 15 two octaves above. Writing the scale of mode m ∈ {major, minor} as sm = (sm[0], …, sm[6]), the map from degree to semitone is

σm(d) = 12 ⁢ ⌊d-17⌋ + sm [(d-1)mod7] (1)

and with the tonic at MIDI note number τ the sounding pitch is π(d) = τ + σm(d). Here smajor = (0,2,4,5,7,9,11) and sminor = (0,2,3,5,7,8,10).

There are two reasons for this representation. First, a fragment becomes independent of key and mode, so the same fragment is reusable in major, in minor, and in any key. Second, the sequence (parallel motion) described below becomes plain addition on degrees. Transposing by semitones would produce notes outside the scale; transposing by degrees never can.

The same degree sequence (fragment m0100), with only the map changed. Not one note is rewritten

2.2Fragments

A fragment f is a finite sequence of notes. A note n = (d, β, δ, v) carries degree, onset beat, duration and velocity, with β ∈ [0, 8) — that is, within two bars. We write its interval sequence as

Δ(f) = ( d2-d1 , … , dk-dk-1 ) (2)

A degree difference of 1 is a second (stepwise motion), 2 a third, and 3 or more a leap of a fourth or wider.

3Induction from a corpus

The coefficients of the scoring function were not chosen freely. They are fitted to statistics measured over 125 public-domain melodies, 1,768 notes, 1,643 intervals, transcribed for this purpose. "Public domain" was taken to mean "the composer died in 1955 or earlier, or the piece is traditional" (the latest death year in the collection is 1952; 26 pieces are traditional). By mode: 93 major, 32 minor.

Two quantities are defined from this distribution: the stepwise ratio and the ratio of leaps that are filled in afterwards.

rstep(f) = |{i:|Δi|=1}| |Δ(f)| , rfill(f) = |{i:|Δi|≥2∧|Δi+1|=1}| |{i:|Δi|≥2}| (3)

The measured values are rstep = 0.691 and rfill = 0.624. The second shows that the vocal principle "after a leap, fill the gap by step" holds in more than six cases out of ten in celebrated melodies. A line that keeps leaping cannot be sung.

3.1Extracting melodic formulas

From the degree sequences of the corpus, relative n-grams are extracted as melodic formulas. A subsequence of length ℓ is identified with its normalised form (0, di+1-di, …), and its frequency becomes its weight. This yields 490 melodic formulas, 26 cadential formulas, 58 soaring figures, and 147 rhythmic cells.

The most consequential is the soaring figure, typified by (0, 3, 2, 1) — rise a fourth, then descend by step. It occurs 189 times across the 125 melodies and creates the climactic moment in many of them.

The extracted formulas themselves, played as relative degrees. These are the raw material of the fragments

4Candidate generation and the scoring function

4.1Generating candidates

Candidate fragments are made by chaining melodic formulas into a degree sequence and assigning rhythmic cells to it. Chaining shares the end of one formula as the start of the next. The only randomness is a seeded mulberry32 generator (a 32-bit multiply-and-xorshift PRNG); Math.random is never used, and string seeds are folded to 32 bits with FNV-1a. Generation is therefore exactly reproducible. The candidate count is N = 300,000.

The beauty score of a fragment f is defined below. It starts from a baseline of 50 and adds each term. P are non-negative penalties; D and R are signed (a bonus inside the band, a penalty outside); B and β are non-negative bonuses.

where T(f) is the set of expressive tags the fragment carries. Pspan penalises range beyond a twelfth (6·(span − 12)+); Ddens adds 10 when note density (notes ÷ 8) falls in 0.9–1.8 and subtracts in proportion to the excursion below 0.7 or above 2.2. The main terms follow.

Penalty for leaps, over degree differences a = |Δi|:

Pleap = ∑i [ 3⁢(ai-3)+ + 2⋅1[ai=0] + 10⋅1[ai-1≥4∧ai≥4] ] + 60⋅(rleap-0.1)+ (5)

The rhythm term Rrhythm maps the number of distinct note values to {1 → −22, 2 → −8, 3 → +6, ≥4 → +12}. A fragment with a single note value sounds like a nursery rhyme however beautiful its intervals are, and can never become a phrase of a piece. Hence the single largest penalty in the function.

5Building the catalogue by stratified selection

Taking the top 999 by score fails. The score is one scale, so the top of it is uniformly "well-behaved", and anyone listening indefinitely will tire of it. Worse is the gap in coverage: if no fragment fits some slot of some chord progression, any piece that draws that progression breaks.

Selection is therefore posed as constrained subset selection. From the candidate set C (|C| = 300,000) choose |X| = 999.

maxX⊆C ∑f∈X S(f) s.t. |X∩Gj| ≥gj , |X∩Kl| ≤kl , |X∩Bq| ≥9 (7)

Bq are buckets: the candidates partitioned into 6 contours × 5 tension levels × 2 cadence classes = 60 strata (10 of which are necessarily empty by definition). Gj are lower bounds and Kl caps.

Table 3. Examples of the lower bounds Gj (excerpt from more than 90)
GoalMinimumIntent

The problem contains multi-cover and is therefore NP-hard. The implementation is greedy, with one ordering decision that matters: coverage goals are filled before quantity goals. Coverage goals (rhythmic types, chord slots) are fine-grained and mutually disjoint; quantity goals (the stepwise band, note counts) can be met by almost any fragment. Fill quantity first and the 999 slots are consumed by similar high-scoring fragments, leaving coverage unmet. In the other order, a fragment taken for coverage satisfies the quantity goals as a side effect. Within each round, the candidate that satisfies the most unmet goals at once is preferred.

The caps Kl are weaker than the bounds: a cap is ignored only when it blocks a lower bound (a skewed catalogue beats a broken piece). The resulting catalogue has a median of 8 notes per fragment, ranging from 6 to 12.

6Precomputing chord compatibility

Deciding at run time whether a fragment fits a chord is expensive. Evaluating 999 fragments × 60 chords × 2 bars measures 329 milliseconds — fifty times the cost of assembling a whole piece (6.5 ms). Compatibility against every member of the chord vocabulary is therefore computed once, per fragment.

Writing PC(c) for the pitch-class set of chord c, a bar B (the first or second half of a fragment) fits c when

fits(B,c) ⇔ (∃n∈B:pc(n)∈PC(c)) ∧ (∀n∈B: exp(n) ∧ pc(n)∉PC(c) ⇒ res(n) ) (8)

where

exp(n) ⇔ (β(n)mod2=0) ∨ (δ(n)≥1.5) res(n) ⇔ |dnext-d|≤1 ∧ pc(nnext)∈PC(c) (9)

In words: a note that is exposed — on a strong beat or held long — is allowed to be a non-chord tone, but only if it resolves by step to a chord tone. This turns the contrapuntal treatment of suspensions and appoggiaturas into a computable predicate. Short non-chord tones on weak beats (passing and neighbour notes) are unconstrained.

For each fragment f, the index sets over the chord vocabulary Vm (33 chords in major, 27 in minor)

Fm(j) (f) = { i : fits(Bj(f),Vm[i]) } , j∈{1,2} (10)

are stored as JSON. At run time, "the fragments that fit these two chords" is a set-membership test. The chords on which a suspension is available are stored the same way.

Because these are stored as indices, the order of existing entries in the chord vocabulary must never change. Inserting in the middle makes every fragment point at a different chord. Additions go at the end.

7Assembling a piece

7.1Form

A piece consists of four sections, A, A′, B, A″. Each is divided into L slots, one slot per fragment (two bars). At the standard 32 bars, L = 4. A, A′, A″ use variants of one chord progression; only B draws a different one.

Within a section, slots are assigned phrase roles (a, a′, b, a″). a is chosen freely; a′ and a″ are sequences of a; b is chosen freely but required to contrast with a.

7.2Sequences

The transposition operator is defined as

Tk(f) = {(d+k,β,δ,v):(d,β,δ,v)∈f} , domain : ∀d:1≤d+k≤15 (11)

Rhythm and velocity are preserved; only degrees move. This is the substance of the sensation that "the same song is still going". The amount k is drawn from a preference table per section and role. The table carries a direction.

a alone, then a followed by T₊₂(a). The rhythm is identical; the pitch rises by a second

k = 0 sits last in every row. Zero is not a sequence: it is literal repetition. Put it first, as an earlier version did, and bars are mass-produced in which the harmony moves while the melody does not change by a single note — 28% of all exactly-identical two-bar fragments came from this.

The chosen amount is not the first one that fits, but the minimum under the lexicographic order

( lift, ⌊ord/3⌋, cad, ord ) (12)

where lift is 1 when a climbing slot fails to reach the required height, ord is the position in the table, and cad ranks the desirability of the closing degree. The point is the second component, which rounds ord into tiers of three: within a tier the cadence decides, across tiers the direction wins. Prefer direction alone and the rate at which a′ "answers" falls from a measured 82.8% to 53.4%; prefer cadence alone and the A→A′ rise disappears. Choosing either one outright always destroys the other.

7.3The intensity curve and the pitch window

Each slot (σ, k) — section index and position — is given a required tension and a pitch window. The raw tension is

ρ(σ,k) = { 3+2k/k∗σ = 2, k < k* 5σ = 2, k = k* 3σ = 2, k > k* uσ+(wσ-uσ)⋅k/(L-1)otherwise (13)

k* is the climax slot: L − 2 when L ≥ 3, otherwise L − 1. It sits one before the last so that something is left to land on. (u, w) is A: (1,2), A′: (2,3), A″: (3,1).

The pitch window, with peak(f) the highest degree of the fragment, is

pmax = { 14climax 10A″ and after the peak 11otherwise , pmin = { 12climax 1otherwise (14)

Since σm is strictly increasing and the non-climax ceiling (11) never meets the climax lower bound (12), it follows structurally that the highest note of the piece appears only in the climax slot. The climax additionally requires the fragment's own peak to occur exactly once, so the highest note sounds exactly once in the whole piece. What moves a listener is not height as such, but the singularity of having arrived there for the first time.

7.4Climbing to the climax

A ceiling alone is not enough: if the bars before the peak stay low, the peak is reached by a leap. Climbing slots are therefore given a preferred lower bound.

rise(2,k) = max(2, 10-(k∗-1-k) ) , 0<k<k∗ (15)

This is a preference, not a constraint; if no candidate satisfies it the system gives up silently (see below). This single change raised the highest note of the bar before the peak from 12.5 to 13.8 semitones above the tonic, and shrank the final step from 9.1 to 6.3 semitones.

7.5The filter cascade

The fragment for a slot is drawn by first forming the candidate set C0 from the hard constraints (chord fit and the pitch window), then narrowing it with preferences in order. Each preference is a monotone, non-emptying filter.

Ci+1 = { φi(Ci)φi(Ci)≠∅ Ciotherwise (16)

Because of this "do not narrow if nothing matches" rule, no number of stacked preferences can empty the set, and the measured rate of falling back to the emergency fragment is zero. The order is not by strength of effect but by what must be applied while the pool is still wide.

  1. Contrast of contour (b differs from a in contour and in onset pattern)
  2. Soaring (climax only)
  3. Climb (equation 15)
  4. Preferred closing degree, by role (a stays open, a″ lands on the tonic)
  5. Breath (phrase ends close; mid-phrase slots do not)
  6. Suspension (over the darkening chords, and just before the peak)
  7. Pentatonic (singability)

Cadence is applied before pentatonic and suspension because, applied later, the already-narrowed set no longer contains the closing degree in question and the filter silently does nothing. For the same reason, suspension precedes pentatonic over the darkening chords.

8Modulation

Only the final section A″ is written with a new tonic. Chord symbols are relative to the tonic, so not one of them is rewritten: only τ moves, and the progression, the phrase structure and the sequences all rise with it. The step is s ∈ {1, 2} semitones.

Drawing the step by a coin flip produces combinations whose key signatures fly apart. From D-flat major (five flats), +1 semitone lands on D major (two sharps), a move of seven positions around the circle of fifths — the farthest possible jump. From the same key, +2 semitones lands on E-flat major (three flats), a distance of two. Writing φ(τ, m) for the position on the circle of fifths, the distance and the weight are

d(s) = |φ(τ+s,m)-φ(τ,m)| , P(s) ∝ (1+d(s))-3 (17)

When both steps are far (d ≥ 5) no lottery is held and the nearer one is taken. The exponent 3 was chosen by measurement: the mean distance and the frequency of the worst case (7) over 3,000 draws are as follows.

Raising the exponent further all but eliminates the +1 step, which would remove the semitone lift — the very cliché of the ballad — from the system.

As a pivot, the last bar of B is replaced by the dominant of the new key. Rising a semitone without warning sounds abrupt; one bar of the dominant lets the ear accept the new key before landing on its tonic. The pivot is omitted when that bar would fall inside the climax slot (in 16-bar pieces). A slot is two bars, so changing key halfway through it could make some other note equal the peak in pitch. The singularity guaranteed by equation 14 outranks the smoothness of the join.

9Voice placement

Fragments are written in degrees, so realising them as pitches needs a tonic. Accompaniment, bass and pad are generated from the harmony independently. The naive implementation places the accompaniment by absolute range, which never looks at where the melody is singing: measurement showed 33.8% of melody notes overtaken by the accompaniment or the pad, by as much as 13 semitones. When the accompaniment crosses above the melody, the roles invert and the melody is buried.

The same eight bars, with only the accompaniment placement changed. Listen for whether the melody comes through

The ceiling in bar b is set from the lowest melody note.

C(b) = minn∈M(b) π(n) - Δ , Δ=2 (18)

A chord V = (v1 < … < vr) is then fitted under the ceiling. The only permitted operations are octave transposition and dropping upper voices; note names are never changed. Pushing a chord down by semitones turns it into a different chord. The octave shift j ∈ ℤ is chosen by the cost

J(j) = 1000⋅(v1+12j+w-C)+ + 10⋅out(j) + |v1+12j-vprev| (19)

where w = vr − v1 is the spread of the chord, out(j) the excursion outside the normal register, and the last term is voice leading against the previous bar. The factor 1000 makes obedience to the ceiling absolute; the normal register comes next, smoothness last. If it still does not fit, upper voices are dropped (at least two are kept).

The pad, which is held for four beats, is additionally checked for semitone friction: tones a semitone away from an exposed melody note in the bar (the exp of equation 9) are removed.

V′ = { v∈V : ¬∃n∈Mexp(b) [(v-π(n))mod12∈{1,11}] } (20)

An arpeggio makes semitone friction vanish in an instant; a sustained tone does not. The seventh of IM7, for example, sits a semitone below the tonic, and while the melody sings the tonic it beats without resolving.

What matters is that this check must not be applied to every semitone clash on a strong beat. In the breakdown taken before equation 20 was introduced, 448 of 559 strong-beat clashes (80%) were non-chord tones, and 99% of those resolved by step to the following note. Those are suspensions and appoggiaturas — precisely the shading this system sets out to produce. Only the remaining 111, where the melody is a chord tone and still muddied, were worth removing. After the change, accidents fall to 11 and 98% of the remaining clashes are resolving suspensions. (That is why the clash rate in Table 5 only goes from 20.1% to 9.8%: the suspensions were not reduced.)

10Performance design

Beats and velocities played as written are mechanical. Dynamic design is not seasoning but skeleton, and is applied regardless of settings. Velocity is the product of three layers.

v(β) = clamp( v0 ⋅ (1+ε) ⋅ L(β) ⋅ W(β) ⋅ K(β) ) (21)
  • L — the section baseline: A: 0.85, A′: 0.95, B: 1.10, A″: 0.80 → 0.55 (falling linearly).
  • W — the swell within a phrase (8 beats), peaking at the phrase's highest note: 1 − 0.18·|p − q| / max(q, 1−q, 0.25).
  • K — the climax curve: 0.88 → 1.20 over the eight beats before the peak, then down to 0.75 over the four beats after. That drop makes the release after a chorus.
  • ε — seed-derived velocity jitter (±10%).
The same twelve bars, with the dynamic design applied and with it flattened

In time, the system applies tenuto (the peak note alone, delayed 30 ms and lengthened by a factor of 1.15), a breath at phrase ends (15 ms), a hesitation before the climax (up to 0.1 beat over two beats), and a ritardando over the last two bars (up to 0.5 beat). All are produced deterministically from the seeded PRNG, so the same song code reproduces even the fluctuations of the performance.

These decisions are also written into the score as notation. Dynamics, hairpins, slurs, tenuto, pedal and rit. all derive from the same L, W, K, so the sound and the score cannot drift apart.

12Limitations

  1. Rhythmic variety is bounded by the arithmetic of the form. Only seven or eight rhythmic types appear across 32 bars. That follows directly from a′ and a″ being sequences of a that preserve rhythm — two types per four slots, across four phrase groups. Break it and the sense that "the same song is still going" goes with it. This is not a defect to be removed.
  2. Motivic return is literal repetition. 58% of the exactly-identical fragments come from motifs returning across sections. That is the structure of verse one, verse two and the final verse of a song; whether it should be varied is a question of style, not of correctness.
  3. The final step to the peak is still a leap. This follows from the explicit demand for a soaring figure — rise a fourth or more, land, descend by step — which was observed 189 times in the corpus. The climb across bars improved, but the leap itself is retained by design.
  4. The harmonic vocabulary is closed under functional harmony. Borrowed chords and secondary dominants are included; modal harmony and non-functional progressions are not. Because fragment compatibility is precomputed over degrees, extending the vocabulary requires regenerating the catalogue.

AppendixThe corpus

Every coefficient of the scoring function, and every melodic formula, was induced from these pieces. "Public domain" was taken to mean "the composer died in 1955 or earlier, or the piece is traditional"; the latest death year in the collection is 1952 (Béla Bartók, d. 1945, is included; John Ireland, d. 1962, is not). 56 composers and 99 pieces, plus 26 traditional pieces: 125 in all. By mode, 93 major and 32 minor.

What was transcribed is, from each piece, the one memorable phrase of the main melody (8–27 beats, median 13). Neither harmony nor accompaniment was taken. What this system learns is the succession of intervals and the rhythmic shapes, and nothing else; the harmonic vocabulary was defined independently, from functional harmony.
